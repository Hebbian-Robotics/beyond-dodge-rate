\begin{abstract}
Dodge rate measures whether a robot avoids an incoming ball, but can miss later
task failure. We study this evaluation gap in simulated humanoid dodgeball,
where task success also requires staying upright and keeping the pelvis within
1 m of its starting position throughout a four-second trial. Controlled camera
interventions test two fixed G1 policies. In a paired 64-seed experiment,
restoring fresh frames at 2 s raises $\pi_1$ success from 50.0\% to 95.3\%, while
dodge rate remains 100\%. In a separate stale-to-blank control, blank input and
fresh-image restoration rescue the same 31 of 64 trials for $\pi_1$ and leave
$\pi_2$ success unchanged. Yet fresh frames bring both policies closer to their
starting position by trial end than blank input does. These simulation findings support reporting
post-encounter motion alongside contact, falls, and area compliance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A humanoid can dodge a ball and still fail its task by falling or leaving the
allowed area (Fig.~\ref{fig:simulation}). Interpreting dodge rate as task success
can therefore hide failures after the encounter. Evaluation must cover the
whole trial.

PAC-MAN evaluates contact and falls, including return to station between
throws~\cite{pacman}. Blackout vs. Freeze shows that similar task success can
conceal different physical consequences of camera faults in
manipulation~\cite{blackout}. We examine this evaluation gap in humanoid evasion.
Paired camera interventions expose two distinctions: unchanged dodge rates can
accompany different area-compliance outcomes, and identical success rates can
accompany different final pelvis distances. A stale-to-blank control asks
whether the observed benefit requires fresh images or also follows removal of
the stale frame. Shared throws and post-hoc scoring analyses test dependence
on the throw distribution and area radius.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Policies, task, and camera input}
We use Russi's G1 Depth Dodge code~\cite{depthcode}, based on
ViBe/ORCS~\cite{vibe} and LeRobot G1 assets~\cite{lerobot}. It combines frozen
SONIC~\cite{sonic} and Theia~\cite{theia} bases with depth adaptation trained by
PPO~\cite{ppo}. $\pi_1$ is the released iteration-4000 model~\cite{depthrelease};
$\pi_2$ was trained separately with the same method, architecture, and recorded
settings. Their incomplete training histories prevent attributing differences
to a training factor. Evaluation fixes both policies and native control settings.
All trials correct an inactive-ball collision bug by disabling collisions while
the ball is parked below the floor and restoring them at launch.

The G1 must dodge a ball, stay upright, and keep its pelvis inside an allowed
area of radius 1 m centred on the pelvis starting position. The ball launches at 1 s;
trials last 4 s or until a fall. The simulator aims at one of six body locations,
adds random position offsets and $\pm30^\circ$ direction variation, and uses a
nominal flight time of 0.55--0.62 s.

Healthy input supplies fresh depth frames.
Stale input repeats the last pre-fault frame, and blank input supplies
zero-valued frames. Faults replace frames before encoding. Theia encodes frames,
and a perception module aggregates 16 visual tokens. The actor combines this
feature with a standing reference and ten-step histories of gyroscope readings,
joint-position offsets, joint velocities, previous actions, and projected
gravity. It produces 29 normalized joint actions for native PD control.
Histories keep updating through faults and switches.

\subsection{Paired experiments}
The input-handling experiment compares healthy, stale, and blank input to test
how faults change dodge rate and area compliance. The fresh-image restoration
experiment tests whether resuming fresh frames changes post-encounter motion.
The restoration timeline is
\begin{center}\footnotesize
launch 1 s $\rightarrow$ fault 1.16 s $\rightarrow$ camera switch 2 s $\rightarrow$ end 4 s
\end{center}
The camera switch follows this fixed schedule rather than being triggered by
throw resolution. Histories keep updating and are not reset. All stale-input
throws resolve by restoration within numerical tolerance. After a separate pilot,
we fixed 64 new seeds and the primary contrast: restoration versus continuous
stale input for $\pi_1$. The other contrasts are secondary.

Within each policy and seed, we match prelaunch robot positions and velocities,
launch time, target, ball position and velocity, and flight time across camera
conditions. We also verify camera schedules. Restoration pairs match recorded
physical traces before 2 s. Missing or mismatched trials halt analysis.
Different prelaunch motion can change the simulator's aim, so equal seeds need
not give equal throws across policies.

The exploratory shared-throw control tests whether the response differences
persist when both policies face the same ball launches. It uses 64 recorded
launches, taking 32 from each policy's healthy-input trials without selecting
for outcome. Both policies receive each initial ball position and velocity
under all five camera conditions. Robot states can still differ, and subsequent
ball trajectories are simulated rather than imposed. This control is analysed
separately and adds no independent training runs.

The stale-to-blank control tests whether the success-rate benefit requires fresh
frames. We rerun the 64 restoration seeds locally, switching at 2 s from stale
input to continued stale input, blank input, or fresh frames. Each triplet
matches throws, supplied frame hashes before 2 s, and recorded pelvis traces
through 2 s. Although runtime input files match archived hashes, rerun
trajectories differ. Every comparison therefore uses the local conditions,
separately from the original restoration estimate. Table~\ref{tab:design}
summarises the cohorts; Supplement A gives selection and matching details.

\subsection{Outcomes and uncertainty}
Task success requires a dodge (a simulator-recorded miss), no fall, and area
compliance throughout the trial. The simulator records a fall when pelvis
height drops below 0.45 m or upright-axis cosine drops below 0.4
(about $66.4^\circ$ tilt). Trials that end before the throw is resolved
(aborted throws) count as failures and remain included in the total trial count.
The area radius is an illustrative task requirement, not an injury threshold
or court rule.

Post-hoc timing checks compare the first sampled area exit with throw
resolution. A hit resolves at contact. A miss resolves at the first control
step at least one second after launch, provided the ball remains active after
contact processing and the fall check. Resolution follows the simulator's
scoring rule rather than measuring closest approach. An exit is late only if
it occurs after resolution; ties count as at-or-before resolution. Samples
are 20 ms apart, without interpolation.

Pelvis distance is horizontal distance from its starting position. Maximum and
final pelvis distances measure excursion and endpoint distance without applying
the area criterion. Post-hoc radius and duration checks reuse all trials,
including hits.

Paired 95\% confidence intervals use 10,000 bootstrap resamples of evaluation
seeds, keeping their conditions together. Intervals are per comparison without
multiplicity adjustment; individual-condition intervals are Wilson intervals.
Evaluation seeds are not independent training runs. Supplement A gives scoring
rules and Supplement B reports additional statistics.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Dodges can hide late area exits}
Under stale input, $\pi_1$ dodges successfully in 121 trials but later leaves the
allowed area. All 122 of its area exits occur after throw resolution. Blank
input also produces late exits, as does $\pi_2$. These timings establish when
area-compliance failures occur, not why the policies differ (Supplement B).

\subsection{Fresh frames change post-encounter motion}
Restoring fresh frames raises $\pi_1$ success from 32/64 to 61/64
(+45.3 percentage points; paired 95\% confidence interval +32.8 to +57.8), while
both conditions dodge 64/64 throws. This rescues 29 trials, with none changing
from success to failure. For $\pi_2$, success changes from 53/64 to 51/64
($-$3.1 percentage points; paired 95\% confidence interval $-$7.8 to 0), with
54/64 dodges in both conditions. No trials improve and two change from success
to failure. No restoration condition has falls or aborted throws. The success differences therefore concern area compliance
after the encounter, without rescuing the initial dodge. Table~\ref{tab:recovery}
reports all conditions; Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery} shows when exits occur.





\subsection{Success-rate increases depend on area radius}
The increase in success rate emerges mainly late in the trial and shrinks with
larger area radii. At four seconds, increasing the radius from 1 to 1.5 m reduces
$\pi_1$'s additional successful trials from 29/64 to 2/64 in the original cohort.
Yet fresh frames reduce its mean maximum pelvis distance from 1.043 to 0.860 m,
independently of that criterion. For $\pi_2$, mean final pelvis distance decreases,
but mean maximum pelvis distance increases slightly in the original cohort.
The shared-throw confidence interval for the change in mean maximum pelvis
distance spans zero. Supplement B gives post-hoc methods and selected results.

\subsection{Blank input has different effects across policies}
Compared with stale input, blank input raises $\pi_1$ success from 70/192 to
171/192 but lowers $\pi_2$ success from 158/192 to 100/192. Both policies stay
in the area more often but dodge less often (Table~\ref{tab:policies}). These
outcomes account for the opposite success-rate changes without explaining
their cause inside the policies.

The response difference persists under shared ball launches. Fresh-image
restoration also raises $\pi_1$ success from 23/64 to 60/64 in that control,
while $\pi_2$ changes from 46/64 to 45/64; dodge counts remain unchanged.
However, the shared-throw confidence interval for $\pi_2$'s blank-versus-stale
effect reaches zero, so this control alone does not establish a harmful
blank-input effect for that policy (Supplement B).

\subsection{Equal success can hide different final distances}
In the local 2 s switch control, fresh and blank input produce identical
success outcomes but different final positions. Fresh frames bring both
policies closer to their starting position than blank input does. Mean final
pelvis distance is lower by 0.295 m for $\pi_1$ (paired 95\% confidence interval
0.253--0.341 m) and 0.117 m for $\pi_2$ (0.091--0.144 m).
The same success outcomes can thus conceal different post-encounter motion.

This endpoint improvement does not establish a reduction in maximum excursion.
For both policies, the paired 95\% confidence interval for the fresh-versus-blank
difference in mean maximum pelvis distance spans zero. These distance summaries
include all 64 trials per policy, including hits. Supplement B gives the
distance statistics; the next subsection examines the success outcomes and
what the blank-input comparison tests.

\subsection{Blank frames reproduce the observed success outcomes}
Restoring fresh frames both supplies current visual information and stops
repeating the stale frame. The stale-to-blank control separates these changes
by replacing the stale frame with zeros at 2 s, after throw resolution, while
retaining the policy histories. It tests whether fresh images are needed for
the observed success-rate benefit at area radius 1 m.

In this separate local control, $\pi_1$ succeeds in 31/64 trials with
continued stale input and 62/64 with either blank input or fresh frames. Both
switches rescue the same 31 trials, with none changing from success to failure.
All 64 throws are dodged under every condition, so these gains come entirely
from preventing area exits. For $\pi_2$, all three conditions succeed on the
same 54/64 trials and dodge 55/64 throws: neither switch changes success.
No falls or aborted throws occur. Blank input thus reproduces the observed
success-rate benefit for $\pi_1$ and the absence of a benefit for $\pi_2$.
Identical outcomes on these seeds do not establish population equivalence.
Supplement B gives all counts and the discordance bound.

The control shows that fresh visual information is not required to reproduce
the observed binary success gain in this setting, although it improves mean
final pelvis distance. It does not show that the policies ignore vision:
histories retain earlier information, and blank frames still pass through the
visual encoder. Nor does it identify the mechanism inside either policy.

\section{Related Work and Limitations}
Our policies remain fixed during testing. Their released training already
includes image corruption and repeated frames~\cite{depthrelease}. VB-Com
switches between vision and blind policies~\cite{vbcom}, while CAP trains
robustness to degraded depth~\cite{cap}. Supplying blank input does not turn a
visual model into a separately trained blind policy. OopsieVerse distinguishes
completion from damage-free completion~\cite{oopsieverse}; our area criterion
measures task compliance rather than damage. Like LIBERO-MAX's matched
execution-time interventions~\cite{liberomax}, our comparisons hold the preceding
trial history fixed within each restoration pair.

The late-switch finding does not establish blank input as a general fallback.
The input-handling experiment supplies blank input from 1.16 s, during the
encounter, whereas the stale-to-blank control switches at 2 s, after throw
resolution. The experiments also use different cohorts and execution
environments. Their results therefore do not provide a paired estimate of the
effect of switching time.

The evidence covers two fixed policies, one simulated robot and starting
posture, the native throw generator, and one recorded launch pool. It does not
establish hardware safety, human/team interaction performance, a universal
fallback, or reliability across training seeds. Pelvis containment does not
ensure whole-body containment. The hard area criterion is additional to the
smooth station-hold training reward, which has weight 0.3 and scale 1 m
(Supplement A). The reward scale does not itself justify that hard criterion.
Training variability is
well documented~\cite{rlrepro}, but our models do not isolate it, and fixed
hyperparameters leave their sensitivity untested. Further work should vary
checkpoints, stance and pre-impact configuration, and test actuator limits and
latency on hardware. Original archives and $\pi_2$ ONNX weights are public;
stale-to-blank records accompany this revision and await public release
(Supplement C).

\section{Conclusion}
A successful dodge can hide a later area-compliance failure. Restoring fresh
frames raises $\pi_1$ success from 32/64 to 61/64 without changing its dodge
count, showing why dodge rate alone is insufficient. In the separate
stale-to-blank control, blank input and fresh-image restoration produce the
same success outcomes, yet fresh frames bring both policies closer to their
starting position by trial end. Overall success can therefore also conceal
differences in motion. These findings from two fixed policies in simulation
support reporting post-encounter motion alongside contact, falls, and area
compliance.
